Abstract

Selective intervention could enable one operator to support multiple autonomous manipulators while collecting demonstrations for policy learning. This requires scene understanding, effective corrections, and timely assistance, yet these requirements are rarely evaluated together. We present Supervise–Intervene–Improve, a risk-informed framework connecting supervision, corrective control, and intervention-data collection. Three independent studies with 72 participants compare Desktop-RGB, VR-RGB, and VR-PointCloud supervision; Kinesthetic Teaching, Motion Controller, and FACTR corrective control; and fleets of one to nine robots. As implemented, VR-PointCloud supported higher recovery and shorter corrections than both RGB interfaces. Kinesthetic Teaching achieved higher recovery than Motion Controller in the primary analysis, qualified by preparation costs and sensitivity checks. Larger fleets reduced coverage and delayed assistance; throughput increased descriptively without a detectable primary omnibus effect. Policies fine-tuned with intervention data achieved higher success rates than the base policies on both manipulation tasks. Together, these findings connect effective supervision and timely corrective assistance with subsequent policy improvement, providing a path toward closing the Supervise–Intervene–Improve loop.

Three within-subjects study designs (Paper Table 1), with 24 participants per cohort. Each participant experienced all conditions of one study. “Both tasks” refers to T-Shape Assembly and Cup Insertion. Manipulation was simulated, with physical input devices; autonomous policies remained fixed during the studies.

Each study used an independent participant cohort. The design supports within-study comparisons but not direct cross-study recovery comparisons or supervision-by-controller interaction claims.

Offline Evaluation

Learning From Intervention

Can the collected corrections improve the autonomous policy?

We fine-tuned policies using Control-study corrections collected under VR-PointCloud: 6,677 OOD frames per interface, sampled at 90% base / 10% correction data for 5,000 updates. Figure 10 reports training seeds 0–2 for both tasks. Supplementary Section S8 documents the protocol and numerical provenance. Selection and reporting reuse validation scores; the results are development estimates.

Cup Insertion used ACT (Fig. 10a). KT had the highest ID mean (88.0 ± 7.2%); MC had the highest OOD mean (30.0 ± 4.0%). T-Shape used a Diffusion policy with an 80-s limit (Fig. 10b). FACTR had the highest ID mean (84.7 ± 5.8%); KT had the highest OOD mean (40.7 ± 2.3%, versus BASE’s 35.3 ± 1.2%). Rankings are descriptive.

Limitations

Our findings reflect two simulated manipulation tasks and the interfaces as implemented. Studies with physical robots, more varied tasks, and longer sessions would help assess broader applicability. Separate participant cohorts support within-study comparisons but do not establish interactions between supervision and control. The findings apply to the tested interface configurations and assistance demands; their applicability to other supervision settings remains to be established.

Conclusion

Supervise–Intervene–Improve connects human oversight with learning from corrective intervention. Across three studies with 72 participants, VR-PointCloud supported higher recovery and shorter corrections, while corrective-control interfaces offered different trade-offs between recovery, preparation, and preference. Increasing concurrent demand reduced access to assistance, showing why correction quality alone cannot characterize effective oversight. Fine-tuning with recorded interventions yielded higher observed policy success on both tasks in development evaluations. Together, these findings motivate interfaces that support scene understanding, effective correction, and timely assistance, while retaining corrections as demonstrations for subsequent policy improvement. This connects assistance during deployment with learning toward more capable autonomy.
